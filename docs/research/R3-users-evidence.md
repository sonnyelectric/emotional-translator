# R3: Who needs Emotional Translator, and the evidence (accessed 2026-11-20)

Method note: found by web search and page fetch on 2026-11-20. Items marked **[search-snippet only]** were not checked against the full text. Items marked **[UNVERIFIED]** should not be said aloud without a check.

## 1. Deaf and hard-of-hearing (DHH) people

### Numbers
- Global: WHO says "By 2050, nearly 2.5 billion people are projected to have some degree of hearing loss" and "over 700 million people - or 1 in every 10 people - will have disabling hearing loss". Disabling = loss greater than 35 dB in the better ear. https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/deafness-and-hearing-loss (page dated 3 Mar 2026; accessed 2026-11-20).
- Global today: more than 1.5 billion people (nearly 20%) live with some hearing loss, 430 million disabling. WHO ear and hearing care page, via search snippet: https://www.who.int/teams/noncommunicable-diseases/sensory-functions-disability-and-rehabilitation/ear-and-hearing-care/hearing-loss-and-the-role-of-health-care-providers **[search-snippet only]**
- WHO lists effects of unaddressed hearing loss: "social isolation, loneliness and stigma" and "limitations in communication and speech" (same fact sheet).
- US: "Approximately 15% of American adults (37.5 million) ages 18 and over report some trouble hearing" (NHIS 2012 data, so dated). By age (NHANES 2015-2020): 10% of ages 55-64, 22% of 65-74, 55% of 75+ have disabling hearing loss. https://www.nidcd.nih.gov/health/statistics/quick-statistics-hearing (updated 2024-09-20; accessed 2026-11-20).
- Caution: "trouble hearing" is self-report, and the figures are not "deaf" counts. Say "people with hearing loss", not "deaf people", in the pitch.

### Evidence captions lose tone
- De Lacerda Pataca et al., CHI 2023, "Visualization of Speech Prosody and Emotion in Captions: Accessibility for Deaf and Hard-of-Hearing Users", doi 10.1145/3544548.3581511. Argues captions lack the emotion and emphasis speech carries. 16 DHH participants compared three prototype caption styles against conventional captions. The emotion-based style beat conventional captions at showing emotion and emphasis, with a moderate cost to legibility. https://researchwith.njit.edu/en/publications/visualization-of-speech-prosody-and-emotion-in-captions-accessibi/ (abstract via search; PDF text not read). A third-party review says the prosody-only style did not beat conventional captions on emphasis **[UNVERIFIED]**. Implication for us: emotion visualization is the part that works, and legibility is the cost, so keep clouds subtle and toggleable.
- Ubur, Gracanin, Rho (Virginia Tech), "Adaptive Captioning with Emotional Cues: Supporting DHH and Neurodivergent Learners in STEM", ACII 2025, arXiv 2603.15977. Real-time captions "often omit emotional and non-verbal cues". 24 completers (8 DHH, 7 ADHD, 1 dyslexia). Some prototypes cut self-reported load and raised comprehension (54% baseline to near 100%), but the authors admit different lectures per condition confound this. Over half of participants wanted toggles. Two Deaf participants said clean text with color or bold beat cartoony icons. https://arxiv.org/html/2603.15977v1 (accessed 2026-11-20). Design lesson: colored, shaped clouds fit better than emoji, and need an off switch.
- Hassan and Burnett: three expressive-caption schemas built from audio emotion detection showed no significant difference in understanding or preference. Their evaluation used hearing participants, not the main user group. https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10442631 **[search-snippet only]**. Honest limit for us: automatic audio-emotion captions have not been shown to help.
- CuCap (ASSETS 2025): abstract says emotion visualization was a universally favored choice while prosody preferences varied across cultures. https://www.dgp.toronto.edu/~suhyeon423/publications/cucap/ **[search-snippet only]**
- Honest gap: published studies are small (5 to 33 people). We can say "early research points the same way", not "proven".

## 2. Autistic people and others who find vocal tone hard to read

- Prevalence (US children): CDC ADDM Network, "About 1 in 31 children were identified with autism spectrum disorder among a 2022 sample of 8-year-olds" (16 communities, not nationally representative). https://www.cdc.gov/autism/data-research/autism-data-visualization-tool.html (accessed 2026-11-20). CDC page on site variation: https://www.cdc.gov/autism/articles/prevalence-varies-across-us-communities.html. I found no CDC adult figure.
- Research is mixed, so do not overgeneralize:
  - Zhang et al. 2021 (Autism), meta-analysis of 23 studies: pooled g = -0.63 for affective prosody recognition, shrinking to -0.26 and non-significant after publication-bias correction. https://bohrium.dp.tech/paper/arxiv/812487552035454977 **[search-snippet only]**
  - Leung et al. 2022 (Developmental Review): deficits for specific emotions (anger, happiness, disgust), larger in adults than children. https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/27113/1/Leung_et_al_2021_Author%20final%20version.pdf **[search-snippet only]**
  - Jertberg and van der Burg 2025 (JADD), 1,239 adults: recognition described as "intact but protracted", meaning autistic adults do well given more time. https://centaur.reading.ac.uk/122172/1/jertberg2025_emotion_oma.pdf **[search-snippet only]**. Captions that persist on screen give more time, which is a fair design argument.
- Double empathy problem: Milton (2012), Disability & Society 27(6):883-887, doi 10.1080/09687599.2012.710008. Reframes autistic/non-autistic misreading as mutual, not a one-sided deficit. https://kar.kent.ac.uk/62639 (via search). Critics say it complements, not replaces, cognitive-empathy accounts.
- Respectful framing: the tool offers an optional second channel for anyone, and does not "fix" autistic people. Do not claim it detects what autistic people "miss". Ask autistic attendees before you say anything about them on stage.

## 3. Alexithymia, language learners, caption-reliant hearing people

- Alexithymia (difficulty identifying and describing feelings): commonly about 10% of the general population, ranges of 5 to 19% depending on sample. A 1999 Finnish population study (n=1,285) found 13%. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10088984/ ; https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychiatry/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2024.1338194/full. Link to reduced recognition of emotion in voices is stated in a Frontiers paper citing two studies; I did not find the primary studies **[search-snippet only]**.
- Non-native listeners: Bhatara et al. 2016 (PLoS ONE) found second-language skill can interfere with recognizing emotion in speech prosody, particularly positive emotions; overall evidence is "mixed" (some work finds no proficiency effect). https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4890767 **[search-snippet only]**. Fits the "multilingual services" track, but frame as a hypothesis to test, not a finding.
- Caption users who are not deaf: UK Ofcom research found about 7.5 million people had used TV subtitles, about 6 million without hearing impairment. https://www.ofcom.org.uk/__data/assets/pdf_file/0016/42442/access.pdf **[search-snippet only; old, 2006-era data]**. A 2026 industry survey (not Ofcom, 3,000 consumers in five countries) says 79% of UK viewers use subtitles at least sometimes. https://www.advanced-television.com/?p=285307 **[search-snippet only, weak source]**. Use only to say "captions are not just for DHH people".

## 4. Other groups

- Cochlear implant (CI) users: pitch perception is severely impaired and prosodic cues are heavily degraded, so vocal emotion recognition is hard. Review by Jiam, Caldwell, Deroche, Chatterjee, Limb; children's CI work by Chatterjee et al. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5937709 ; https://frontiersin.org/article/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.02190/full **[search-snippet only]**. Strong fit: CI users hear speech but lose the tone cues, exactly what the clouds restore. Caveat for the pitch: the model must also work on the audio a CI user cannot hear well, which it does because it reads the signal, not the ear.
- Neurodivergent learners (ADHD, dyslexia) were in the Ubur et al. study (section 1). Auditory processing disorder: I did not search for it; **[no evidence gathered]**.

## 6. Ethics and framing

- Barrett, Adolphs, Marsella, Martinez, Pollak (2019), Psychological Science in the Public Interest 20(1):1-68, doi 10.1177/1529100619832930. Reviewed 1,000+ findings; expression of six common emotions varies across cultures, situations and people. They criticize emotion-detection software built on the "common view". The paper is about faces, not voices, so cite it for the general principle only. A companion line of work (Cowen, Keltner et al.) argues voice and context also carry signal; I did not verify details. https://www.psychologicalscience.org/publications/emotional-expressions-reconsidered-challenges-to-inferring-emotion-from-human-facial-movements.html
- EU AI Act, Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Article 5(1)(f) prohibits "the use of AI systems to infer emotions of a natural person in the areas of workplace and education institutions", "except where the use of the AI system is intended to be put in place or into the market for medical or safety reasons". Prohibitions apply from 2 February 2025 (Art. 113(a), per https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/5/, accessed 2026-11-20; that site says some amended points apply 2 December 2026, which I did not trace; point (f) is shown as unamended). I did not read the Official Journal text.
  - Open issue: FPF reads the ban as covering emotions, not intentions; the Commission Guidelines reportedly cover intentions too (Freshfields summary). https://fpf.org/blog/red-lines-under-eu-ai-act-unpacking-the-prohibition-of-emotion-recognition-in-the-workplace-and-education-institutions/ **[search-snippet only]**
  - Why it matters to us: a classroom or workplace deployment in the EU could fall under the ban. Not legal advice. Two things help: the user can choose it for themselves as an accessibility aid, and it shows a cue about the speaker's voice, not a score on a named person. But whether that avoids the ban is a legal question we have not answered. Safest hackathon line: "we have not assessed EU deployment; schools and workplaces would need legal review."
- Responsible framing for the pitch:
  1. Say "vocal cues detected", never "emotions felt" or "true feelings".
  2. Show uncertainty (soft edges, confidence, a "may be" wording) and let the viewer toggle clouds off.
  3. Controlled by the person reading, not by an employer or teacher monitoring a speaker; no storing or scoring of individuals.
  4. State limits: cultural and individual variation, non-native accents, and neurodivergent and disabled speakers whose voices may be misclassified by a model trained on typical speech **[general concern, no source gathered]**.
  5. Word-versus-voice "mismatch" flag: phrase as "tone and words differ here", never "this person is lying/sarcastic".
  6. Lead with consent in demos: use team members' own recordings.

## 7. Five interview questions for today (2 to 3 attendees; ask permission before quoting)

1. "Think of the last time you read captions in a meeting or video. Was there a moment you could not tell how someone meant what they said? What happened next?"
2. (Show the demo, no explanation.) "What do you think this cloud is telling you? What would you do with it?"
3. "When would you want this on, and when would you turn it off? Who should never be able to see it?"
4. "Where would this get it wrong, or make you trust it too much? What would make you stop using it?"
5. "If it is wrong half the time, is it still useful? What would it need to show so you know how sure it is?"
